How to Find Your True Running Shoe Size Across Brands

Pulling on a pair of running shoes in your usual size only to find your toes jammed against the front, or your heel swimming in extra space, is a common frustration. The reason has less to do with your feet changing and more to do with how differently brands build their shoes. Understanding how to measure your feet and how sizing actually works can save you from blisters, black toenails, and returns.

Measuring your feet the right way

Most people measure their feet once as a teenager and never again, but foot length and width can change with age, weight, and pregnancy, so it's worth remeasuring every few years. Stand on a piece of paper (don't sit, since feet flatten and lengthen under body weight when standing) and trace both feet, since almost everyone has one foot slightly longer than the other. Measure the longest point of each tracing from heel to the tip of the longest toe, which isn't always the big toe.

Do this measurement in the evening or after a workout, not first thing in the morning, because feet swell throughout the day and during exercise as fluid shifts into the tissue. A morning measurement can read up to a full size smaller than what your feet actually need for a run later that day. Always size shoes to your larger foot and note the width at the ball of the foot (the widest part), not just the length, since two feet with identical length can need very different width fittings.

Why the same size number fits differently across brands

Shoe size is only a rough proxy for the actual three-dimensional shape a brand builds its shoes around, called the last. Two brands can both label a shoe "size 10," but if one uses a curved last with a narrower toe box and the other uses a straighter, wider last, the internal volume and shape of those two size-10 shoes can differ by half a size or more in usable space. This is also why a brand's trail shoe and its road racing flat, even in the same nominal size, often fit differently, since different shoe categories within one brand may use different lasts.

Country sizing systems compound the confusion, since US, UK, and EU size scales don't convert with a fixed offset the way many charts imply. EU sizing (Paris point sizing) is based on the length of the last in units of two-thirds of a centimeter, while US and UK sizing use different base measurements and half-size increments, so rounding errors accumulate. The only truly universal reference point is foot length in centimeters or millimeters, sometimes labeled as Mondopoint sizing, and checking a brand's specific size chart against your measured foot length in centimeters is far more reliable than converting from a size you wore in a different brand.

Sizing up for running specifically

Running shoes are typically sized with more room than casual or dress shoes because of how feet behave during exercise. Feet can swell measurably during a run, especially on longer efforts or in hot weather, and repeated impact pushes the foot slightly forward inside the shoe with every stride, particularly on descents. This is why the common advice for running shoes is to leave a thumb's width, roughly 3/8 to 1/2 inch (about 1 to 1.2 cm), of space between your longest toe and the end of the toe box when standing.

Many runners find they need to go up half a size, sometimes a full size, from their everyday casual shoe size specifically for running shoes, and this is normal rather than a sign of buying the wrong size. Insufficient toe room is one of the most common causes of black or bruised toenails on longer runs, since the toenail repeatedly strikes the front of the shoe on downhill or braking strides. Width matters just as much as length here: a shoe that's long enough but too narrow will cause the foot to slide forward on impact even with adequate toe-box length, recreating the same jamming problem.

Reading and comparing size charts

When trying a new brand, start from your measured foot length in centimeters rather than the size you wear elsewhere, and look up that centimeter measurement on the specific brand's own size chart rather than relying on a generic conversion table. Brands publish these charts because their own internal sizing doesn't reliably match industry conventions, and the chart reflects actual last measurements for that specific shoe line rather than a theoretical size equivalence.

Also check whether the brand offers width options (commonly labeled B, D, 2E, or narrow/regular/wide), since standard running shoes are usually built on a "D" last for men and "B" for women, and a foot that's wider or narrower than that standard will fit better in a dedicated width option even at the correct length. If a brand doesn't offer width options, comparing the stated toe-box shape (rounded, tapered, or foot-shaped/wide) in reviews or fit guides can help predict whether that specific model will accommodate a wider or narrower forefoot.

FAQ

Should I measure my feet standing up or sitting down?
Measure standing, since body weight flattens and slightly lengthens the foot compared to sitting, giving a more accurate result for how your foot behaves when bearing weight during a run.
How much space should there be between my toes and the front of a running shoe?
A common guideline is about a thumb's width, roughly 3/8 to 1/2 inch (1 to 1.2 cm), between the longest toe and the end of the toe box to allow for swelling and forward foot movement during runs.
Why do I wear a different size in running shoes than in casual shoes?
Running shoes are typically built with extra length and volume to accommodate foot swelling during exercise and forward slide on impact, so many people need a half size or full size larger than their everyday shoe size.
Is foot length the only measurement that matters for sizing?
No, width at the ball of the foot matters just as much as length; two feet with the same length can require very different width fittings, and a shoe that's long enough but too narrow can still cause fit problems.
