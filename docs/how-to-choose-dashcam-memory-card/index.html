How to Choose a Dashcam Memory Card (and Avoid Loop-Recording Failures)

Dashcams don't use memory cards the way phones or regular cameras do. Because loop recording constantly overwrites old footage, a card that looks fine on paper can fail within months, leaving you with corrupted clips or a camera stuck on a formatting error right when you need footage most.

Why Ordinary microSD Cards Wear Out in a Dashcam

A typical consumer microSD card is designed around a write-once, read-many usage pattern: you take a photo or clip, it gets written once, and you view it dozens of times afterward. Loop recording inverts this completely. The card is written to almost continuously, in a tight rotation of files that get deleted and rewritten every few minutes, which concentrates an enormous number of write cycles onto the same physical NAND blocks over and over.

Flash memory cells degrade with every write cycle, and cheaper consumer cards typically use NAND optimized for occasional writes rather than sustained, cyclical overwriting. Once a block wears past its rated cycle count, it can no longer reliably hold data, which shows up as corrupted video files, audio/video desync, or the camera reporting a card error and demanding a reformat. This is why a card that's technically "fast enough" can still die in three to six months of dashcam use.

Cards explicitly marketed for dashcam, security-camera, or drone use are built on NAND rated for continuous overwrite endurance, usually specified in either total terabytes written (TBW) or an hours-of-continuous-recording rating. That endurance spec, not the storage capacity, is the number that predicts how long the card will survive in a loop-recording device.

The Speed Rating That Actually Matters (and the One That Doesn't)

Manufacturers print several different speed ratings on a microSD card, and not all of them are relevant to video recording. The A1 and A2 "application performance" ratings measure random read/write IOPS, which matters for running apps directly off the card on a phone — it has little bearing on a dashcam's ability to write a continuous video stream.

The rating that actually matters for dashcams is the UHS Speed Class or Video Speed Class, shown as U1/U3 or V10/V30/V60/V90. These guarantee a minimum sustained sequential write speed (U3 and V30 both guarantee at least 30 MB/s sustained), which is what a video sensor needs to keep up with continuous encoding without dropping frames or corrupting the current file. A camera recording at higher resolutions or bitrates, or from multiple channels (front and cabin), needs a card rated V30 or higher to avoid buffer overruns.

Capacity also indirectly affects lifespan: a larger card takes longer to fill up, so it completes fewer full overwrite loops per month than a small card recording the same amount of footage. A larger high-endurance card genuinely wears out slower than a small one of the same rated endurance, simply because each block gets rewritten less frequently.

File System and Formatting Habits That Prevent Corruption

Cards larger than 32 GB are normally formatted in exFAT rather than FAT32, and most dashcams handle this automatically — but a card that was previously formatted on a computer for a different file system can behave unpredictably until it's reformatted inside the camera itself. Always let the dashcam do the initial format rather than assuming a factory-fresh card is already compatible.

Over time, the constant creation and deletion of loop-recording files fragments the file allocation table, which is part of why most dashcam manufacturers recommend a full in-camera reformat every one to two weeks rather than only when problems appear. This resets the file system's bookkeeping and gives the card's wear-leveling controller a cleaner picture of which blocks are in rotation.

Regularly pulling the card out to back up important clips and then reformatting is a cheap habit that meaningfully extends both the readability of your footage and the physical lifespan of the card.

FAQ

Can I use a regular microSD card from a phone in my dashcam?
You can, but it will likely wear out faster because it isn't built for continuous overwrite cycles; a card rated for endurance or dashcam/surveillance use will last significantly longer under the same recording load.
Do A1 or A2 ratings matter for dashcam performance?
Not much. A1/A2 measure random-access application performance, while sustained sequential writes for video are governed by the UHS or Video Speed Class (U3/V30 or higher), which is the rating to prioritize.
How often should I reformat a dashcam memory card?
Most manufacturers recommend a full in-camera reformat every one to two weeks to clear file system fragmentation and reduce the chance of corrupted loop-recording files.
What size card should I use for a dashcam?
Larger capacity generally means longer lifespan for the same endurance rating, since each memory block is rewritten less frequently, but the endurance rating itself matters more than raw capacity.
