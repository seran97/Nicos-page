UPF Rash Guards and Baby Sun Protection, Explained

Rash guards and swimsuits labeled UPF 50+ have become standard for babies and young children at the beach and pool. Sun-protective clothing works well and avoids the need to cover large areas of delicate skin in sunscreen, but labels can be confusing and no garment protects everything. Understanding what a UPF rating measures, how fit and wear change it, and how clothing fits into broader sun safety guidance helps parents choose swimwear that protects skin while staying comfortable and safe in the water.

What a UPF Rating Measures

UPF stands for ultraviolet protection factor, a rating for fabrics that measures how much ultraviolet radiation, both UVA and UVB, passes through the material. A fabric rated UPF 50 allows roughly one-fiftieth of UV radiation through, blocking about 98 percent. In the United States, sun-protective clothing is commonly tested under a laboratory method from the American Association of Textile Chemists and Colorists and labeled following an ASTM standard, which groups ratings as good for UPF 15 to 24, very good for 25 to 39 and excellent for 40 to 50 or higher. UPF differs from SPF. SPF is measured on skin and applies to sunscreens, primarily reflecting protection against sunburn caused by UVB, while UPF measures transmission through fabric across both UVA and UVB. Ordinary clothing also offers some protection, but the amount varies widely. A light, loosely woven cotton shirt may provide much less protection than people expect, and many fabrics lose protection when wet or stretched. A tested UPF label tells you the fabric has been measured rather than estimated, which is the main reason to look for it on swimwear that will be wet and stretched for hours.

Fit, Fabric and Coverage: What Changes Protection

The rating applies to the fabric as tested, and real-world conditions can reduce it. Stretching opens the spaces between fibers, so a rash guard that is too small and pulled tight over a growing child may let more UV through than its label suggests. Choose a fit that is snug enough to stay in place in the water but not strained across the shoulders and back. Tightly woven or knit synthetics such as polyester and nylon blends with spandex tend to hold their protection when wet better than cotton. Darker or more saturated colors generally absorb more UV than pale ones of the same fabric. Coverage matters as much as the rating: long sleeves, a higher neckline and swim leggings or longer shorts protect more skin than a short-sleeve top. A wide-brimmed or legionnaire-style hat with a neck flap covers the face, ears and neck, which clothing alone misses. Follow the care label, since rinsing out chlorine and salt water and avoiding high heat helps the garment last. Replace swimwear that is thinning, faded or stretched out, because worn fabric can transmit noticeably more UV than when it was new.

Sun Safety Guidance for Babies by Age

The American Academy of Pediatrics advises keeping infants younger than 6 months out of direct sunlight as much as possible, using shade from trees, umbrellas or stroller canopies, along with lightweight clothing that covers the arms and legs and a brimmed hat. For this age group, when adequate shade and clothing are not available, a small amount of sunscreen with an SPF of at least 15 may be applied to small exposed areas such as the face and backs of the hands. Babies this young also overheat easily, so watch for fussiness, flushing or sweating and move to a cooler spot. For children 6 months and older, the AAP recommends broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 15 or higher, with many dermatologists preferring SPF 30 or higher, applied to all skin not covered by clothing. Mineral sunscreens containing zinc oxide or titanium dioxide are often chosen for sensitive skin. Reapply about every two hours and after swimming or sweating, even if the product is labeled water-resistant. Plan outdoor time to avoid the hours when UV is strongest, generally from about 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., and check the local UV index.

Safety Details to Check When Buying Kids Swimwear

Beyond UV protection, a few design details affect safety in and around the water. Avoid drawstrings at the hood or neck of rash guards, cover-ups and swim jackets; federal guidance treats neck and hood drawstrings on children's upper outerwear as a strangulation hazard, and the risk is greater around play equipment and slides. Check that zippers have a fabric guard so they do not pinch skin under the chin, and that decorative bows, buttons or sequins are firmly attached, since small items that come loose can be a choking hazard for babies. Visibility is worth considering too. Bright, high-contrast colors are generally easier to spot in water than light blue, white or colors that blend into the pool or sea, which can help a supervising adult keep eyes on a child. For babies who are not toilet trained, pair swimwear with a swim diaper as required by many pools. Finally, remember that sun-protective swimwear is not flotation gear. It provides no buoyancy, and close supervision and, where appropriate, a properly fitted US Coast Guard-approved life jacket remain essential.

FAQ

Is UPF 50+ swimwear enough without sunscreen?
UPF 50+ fabric protects the skin it covers very well, but it leaves the face, hands, feet and sometimes the neck and legs exposed. For children 6 months and older, apply broad-spectrum sunscreen to any uncovered skin and add a brimmed hat. For babies under 6 months, rely on shade and clothing first, using sunscreen only on small areas when needed.
Does a regular cotton T-shirt protect a child from the sun?
It offers some protection, but often much less than a tested UPF garment, especially if the fabric is thin, light-colored or loosely woven. Cotton also tends to lose protection when wet and becomes heavy in the water. For swimming and long beach days, a rash guard with a tested UPF rating is a more reliable choice.
Do UPF rash guards lose effectiveness over time?
They can. Repeated stretching, chlorine, salt water, abrasion from sand and sun exposure gradually wear fabric, and thinning or faded garments may let more UV through. Rinse swimwear in fresh water after use, follow care instructions, avoid high-heat drying and replace items that look worn, stretched or noticeably thinner, especially as children outgrow them.
Can babies under 6 months wear sunscreen?
The AAP's first recommendation for babies under 6 months is to keep them out of direct sun using shade, protective clothing and a brimmed hat. When that is not possible, a small amount of sunscreen with at least SPF 15 can be applied to small exposed areas like the face and backs of the hands. Ask your pediatrician if you have concerns.
