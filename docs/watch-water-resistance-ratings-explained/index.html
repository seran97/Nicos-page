Watch Water Resistance Ratings Explained: 3 ATM to 20 ATM

The water resistance figure printed on a watch dial or case back is one of the most misread specifications in accessories. A watch marked 30 meters is not designed to be taken 30 meters underwater, and a rating that was accurate on the day of purchase can quietly stop being true a few years later. Knowing how these ratings are tested, what each tier is realistically suited for, and which habits break a seal helps you choose the right watch and avoid a fogged or flooded movement.

How Water Resistance Ratings Are Measured

Water resistance is expressed in three interchangeable ways: meters, bar, or ATM (atmospheres). One bar or one ATM corresponds to roughly 10 meters of static water pressure, so 5 ATM, 5 bar, 50 m and WR50 all describe the same rating. The key word is static. The figure describes the pressure a stationary watch withstood in a test, not the depth a person can safely take it while moving.

The main reference for everyday watches is ISO 22810, which sets out testing for watches sold as water resistant, including immersion, condensation and pressure checks. Dive watches are covered by a separate and stricter standard, ISO 6425, which requires at least 100 meters plus extra requirements such as a timing bezel, legibility in darkness, and resistance to shock and magnetism. A watch marked 200 m without dive-specific features meets a pressure figure, but it is not automatically a certified diver.

Real-world use adds dynamic pressure. Swinging an arm through water, diving into a pool, or being hit by a wave briefly raises the load on the seals. That gap between test conditions and real life is why manufacturers recommend activities far shallower than the number on the dial.

What Each Rating Is Realistically Suited For

Manufacturers publish their own guidance, and the owner's manual should always take priority, but the common tiers line up fairly consistently across the industry.

A 3 ATM or 30 m rating covers everyday splashes: rain, washing hands and accidental spills. It is not intended for swimming or showering. Many dress watches and minimalist designs fall into this category.

A 5 ATM or 50 m rating handles brief immersion and, in many manufacturers' guidance, light surface swimming, though some still advise against swimming at this level. Many smartwatches carry this rating.

A 10 ATM or 100 m rating is generally considered the practical minimum for regular swimming, snorkeling and surface water sports. Most sports and field watches aim for this level.

A 20 ATM or 200 m rating suits high-speed water sports, surfing and skin diving, and is common on rugged digital and sport models. For scuba diving, the safer choice is a watch explicitly certified to ISO 6425 and labeled as a diver's watch.

A watch with no water resistance marking at all should be kept away from water entirely.

Why Water Resistance Fades Over Time

Water resistance depends on rubber or synthetic gaskets compressed around the case back, crystal, crown and pushers. These seals are consumable parts. Over time they dry out, flatten and lose elasticity, especially when exposed to heat, sunscreen, perfume, chlorine and salt. A watch rated 100 m when new may resist far less pressure after several years without service.

Mechanical events also compromise seals. Opening the case to replace a battery can disturb or pinch the case-back gasket, which is why reputable repairers reseal and often pressure-test the watch afterward. A hard knock can shift the crystal slightly, and a crown left pulled out, or a screw-down crown left unscrewed, gives moisture a direct path inside.

Heat is a specific threat. Hot showers, saunas and hot tubs make metal and gaskets expand at different rates, and steam penetrates small gaps more easily than liquid water. Moving a warm watch into cold water can also create condensation inside the case. Many service centers suggest a pressure test about once a year for watches worn in water regularly, and always after the case has been opened.

Habits That Keep a Watch Sealed

A few simple rules prevent most water damage. Never pull out the crown or press chronograph pushers underwater unless the manual explicitly allows it, since operating them opens a path through the seal. If the watch has a screw-down crown, confirm it is screwed fully in before every swim.

After swimming in the sea or a chlorinated pool, rinse the watch under cool fresh water and dry it with a soft cloth. Salt crystals and chlorine residue accelerate gasket wear and can corrode bracelets and pins. Leather straps should be kept dry altogether, because water and sweat stiffen and crack leather regardless of the case rating.

Avoid wearing a non-diver watch in hot tubs and saunas, and let a warm watch cool to room temperature before plunging it into cold water. If fog appears under the crystal, moisture is already inside; take the watch to a repairer promptly rather than waiting for it to clear, because trapped humidity corrodes movements and dials. Finally, ask for a pressure test whenever the battery is changed, which is the most practical moment to replace aging gaskets.

FAQ

Can I shower with a 5 ATM watch?
Some manufacturers permit it, but hot water, steam and soap are hard on gaskets, and pressing buttons under the spray adds risk. If the manual is silent or advises against it, removing the watch before showering is the safest habit, especially on older watches whose seals have not been serviced recently.
Is a water resistant watch the same as waterproof?
No. Watch standards avoid the word waterproof because no watch stays sealed indefinitely; gaskets age and can be disturbed by impacts or battery changes. Water resistant means the watch passed pressure testing at a specified level when made. Its real protection depends on its condition, service history and how it is used.
What rating do I need for swimming laps?
For regular swimming, 10 ATM (100 m) is the widely recommended practical minimum, because arm strokes and pool push-offs create dynamic pressure beyond the static test level. A 20 ATM rating adds margin for diving into pools, surfing and snorkeling. Check that any screw-down crown is fully closed before getting in.
How often should water resistance be tested?
For watches worn in water regularly, many service centers suggest a pressure test about once a year. Any watch should also be tested after the case back is opened, such as for a battery change, and after a hard impact. The test is quick and many repairers can do it with dry air pressure equipment.
