Espresso Machine Bar Pressure Explained: 9 vs 15 vs 20 Bar

Espresso machine boxes often advertise 15, 19, or 20 bars of pressure as if a higher number guarantees better coffee. In practice, the pressure printed on the box is usually the maximum the pump can produce, not the pressure that reaches the coffee during brewing. Knowing what a bar is, why roughly 9 bar has long been the reference point for espresso, and which other features actually control the shot helps shoppers compare machines on what matters.

What a Bar of Pressure Means

A bar is a unit of pressure roughly equal to the average atmospheric pressure at sea level; one bar is about 14.5 pounds per square inch. When an espresso machine is described as brewing at 9 bar, it means water is pushed through the coffee at about nine times atmospheric pressure, or around 130 psi.

That pressure is what separates espresso from other brewing methods. Drip coffee relies on gravity, and a French press relies on steeping. Espresso forces hot water through a tightly packed bed of finely ground coffee, called a puck, in a short time. The high pressure lets water penetrate the dense puck, extract soluble flavor compounds quickly, and emulsify coffee oils, which produces the thick body and the layer of foam known as crema. Without enough pressure, water cannot pass through a properly fine grind at a useful rate; with far too much, it tends to find weak spots in the puck and rush through them, a fault called channeling.

Why 9 Bar Is the Long-Standing Standard

Around 9 bar at the coffee puck has been the traditional reference for espresso for decades, and it remains the typical setting on commercial machines in cafes. It is a practical balance: high enough to push water through a fine, compacted dose in a reasonable time, and low enough that the puck stays intact and extracts evenly.

A conventional starting point for a double shot is to extract about twice the weight of liquid espresso as dry coffee used, for example around 18 grams of coffee yielding around 36 grams of espresso, in roughly 25 to 30 seconds, with water at about 90 to 96 degrees Celsius (195 to 205 degrees Fahrenheit). These are starting guidelines rather than rules, and baristas adjust them by taste. Some modern machines also allow pressure profiling, such as a gentle low-pressure pre-infusion that wets the puck before full pressure is applied, which can reduce channeling. The key point is that good espresso depends on stable, appropriate pressure at the puck, not on the highest possible number.

What 15 or 20 Bar Ratings Really Mean

Many home machines use small vibratory pumps that are rated at 15 to 20 bar. That figure describes the pump's maximum capability, typically measured against a blocked outlet, not the pressure used to brew. During a real shot, the pressure at the puck depends on the resistance created by the grind size, dose, and tamp.

Well-designed machines include an over-pressure valve, often abbreviated OPV, that bleeds off excess pressure so the puck sees a level closer to the brewing target. Some budget machines have no OPV or one set high, and they rely instead on the coffee itself or on special baskets to regulate flow. A higher pump rating can provide headroom so pressure does not sag during a shot, but it does not, by itself, make the espresso better. When comparing machines, a pressure gauge, an adjustable or properly set OPV, and stable water temperature are more informative than the headline bar figure.

Pressurized vs Non-Pressurized Baskets

The filter basket that holds the coffee has a large influence on how pressure behaves. A non-pressurized, or single-wall, basket has many small holes across its base. The coffee puck alone creates the resistance, so grind size, dose, and tamping must be dialed in carefully, but the user gains full control over extraction and flavor.

A pressurized, or dual-wall, basket has a second inner wall with a single tiny exit hole. That small opening creates resistance artificially, so the machine builds pressure even with coarser pre-ground coffee or an uneven tamp. The trade-off is that the foam it produces is partly created by forcing coffee through the pinhole, and it can look like crema without the same depth of flavor. Pressurized baskets are forgiving for beginners or anyone without a capable grinder. Non-pressurized baskets reward those who own a burr grinder that can grind fine enough for espresso and who want to fine-tune shots. Many machines ship with both types, which allows starting with one and moving to the other later.

FAQ

Does a 20 bar machine make stronger espresso than a 15 bar one?
Not inherently. Both figures describe maximum pump output, and good shots are generally pulled at a much lower pressure at the puck. Strength comes mainly from the coffee-to-water ratio and extraction, which are controlled by dose, grind size, and shot time. A well-regulated machine with a lower pump rating can produce better espresso than a poorly regulated one with a higher rating.
How can you tell what pressure a machine is actually brewing at?
A built-in pressure gauge on the group head or front panel shows brewing pressure during a shot. Machines without one can be tested with an aftermarket portafilter pressure gauge. Without measurement, the shot itself is the best indicator: very fast, pale shots suggest too little resistance, while slow, dripping, dark shots suggest the grind is too fine or the dose too large.
Is steam pressure the same as brewing pressure?
No. Steam for frothing milk comes from a boiler or thermoblock heated above the boiling point, and its pressure is far lower than the brewing pressure, often around one to two bar in machines that display it. Steam pressure affects how quickly and finely milk can be textured, while brewing pressure affects how water moves through the coffee puck.
Why does espresso run too fast even at full pressure?
A fast shot means the puck is not creating enough resistance, so the pump never builds proper pressure. Common causes are a grind that is too coarse, too little coffee in the basket, an uneven or light tamp, or stale coffee. Grinding finer is usually the first adjustment. If the grinder cannot go finer, a pressurized basket may be the practical alternative.
