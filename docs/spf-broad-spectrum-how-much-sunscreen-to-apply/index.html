SPF and Broad Spectrum Explained: How Much Sunscreen to Apply

The number on a sunscreen bottle is one of the most misunderstood figures in skincare. Many shoppers assume SPF 50 is almost twice as strong as SPF 30, that broad spectrum is a marketing phrase, or that a single morning application lasts all day. In reality, the label reflects specific laboratory tests, and the protection you get at home depends heavily on how much product you use and how often you reapply. Understanding what the label measures helps you choose a formula and use it the way it was tested.

What the SPF Number Actually Measures

SPF, or sun protection factor, measures how well a sunscreen protects against UVB radiation, the part of sunlight mainly responsible for sunburn. In the standardized test used for labeling in the United States, sunscreen is applied at a thickness of 2 milligrams per square centimeter of skin, and the amount of UV needed to cause redness on protected skin is compared with unprotected skin. The scale is not linear. A properly applied SPF 15 filters roughly 93 percent of UVB rays, SPF 30 about 97 percent, and SPF 50 about 98 percent. No sunscreen blocks 100 percent, which is why the FDA does not allow terms such as sunblock on labels. The jump from SPF 30 to SPF 50 is therefore modest on paper, but it can matter in practice because most people apply far less than the tested amount, and a higher SPF leaves more margin for that under-application. Dermatology organizations such as the American Academy of Dermatology generally recommend a broad spectrum SPF of 30 or higher for everyday use. SPF says nothing about UVA protection by itself, which is where the broad spectrum label comes in.

What Broad Spectrum Means on a Label

UVA rays penetrate deeper into the skin than UVB, pass through window glass, and contribute to photoaging such as wrinkles and uneven pigmentation, as well as to skin cancer risk. Under FDA rules, a sunscreen sold in the United States can only be labeled broad spectrum if it passes a standardized test showing that its protection extends across the UVA range in proportion to its UVB protection, measured as a critical wavelength of at least 370 nanometers. Only products that are both broad spectrum and SPF 15 or higher may state that, when used as directed with other sun protection measures, they decrease the risk of skin cancer and early skin aging. A product that is not broad spectrum, or any sunscreen below SPF 15, can only claim to help prevent sunburn. Other regions use different systems, such as the PA rating common in Asian markets or the circled UVA logo used in Europe, so imported products may carry different markings. When comparing products on a shelf, look for both broad spectrum and an SPF of 30 or above rather than focusing only on the largest number.

How Much Sunscreen to Apply

The tested amount of 2 milligrams per square centimeter is much thicker than most people use, and research on real-world habits consistently finds that people apply only a fraction of it. Because protection drops as the layer gets thinner, under-application can leave you with considerably less protection than the label suggests. A widely used guideline for the whole body of an adult in a swimsuit is about one ounce, roughly the amount that fills a shot glass. For the face and neck, a common rule of thumb is about a quarter to a half teaspoon, or two full strips of lotion along the length of the index and middle fingers. Sticks and sprays are especially easy to under-apply. With a stick, several back-and-forth passes over each area are usually needed, followed by rubbing in. With sprays, spray until the skin glistens and rub it in, avoid inhaling the mist, and spray into your hands for the face rather than directly onto it. Tinted moisturizers, foundations and facial mists with SPF can add protection, but people rarely use enough of them to reach the labeled SPF, so they work best layered over a dedicated sunscreen rather than replacing it.

Reapplication and Water Resistance

Sunscreen does not last all day. It is rubbed off by hands, clothing and towels, broken down by sweat and water, and some filters degrade with sun exposure. The FDA labeling directions say to reapply at least every two hours, and immediately after swimming, sweating heavily or towel drying. Water resistance is regulated as well. A label may say water resistant for 40 minutes or 80 minutes, meaning the product kept its stated SPF after that amount of time in water during testing. Claims such as waterproof or sweatproof are not permitted in the United States because no sunscreen meets them. Once the stated time has passed, protection should be assumed to be reduced and the product reapplied. On office days with little sun exposure, a morning application may be adequate for many people, though time near windows or outdoors at lunch can justify a top-up. Sunscreen is also only one part of sun protection. Shade during peak midday hours, a wide-brimmed hat, UV-blocking sunglasses and protective clothing all reduce exposure. Check the expiration date, since sunscreens are generally formulated to stay stable for about three years, and discard any product that has separated or changed in smell or texture.

FAQ

Is SPF 100 much better than SPF 50?
The difference in UVB filtering between SPF 50 and higher numbers is small when both are applied at the tested amount. A very high number can also create a false sense of security that leads people to stay in the sun longer or skip reapplying. Applying enough product, choosing broad spectrum protection and reapplying every two hours matter more than chasing the highest SPF.
Can I mix two sunscreens to get a higher SPF?
No. SPF values do not add together, so layering an SPF 30 sunscreen with an SPF 15 foundation does not create SPF 45. Your protection is roughly that of the highest-rated product, provided you applied it in a sufficient amount. Mixing sunscreen into moisturizer or makeup can also dilute it and disrupt the even film it needs to work properly.
Do I need sunscreen on cloudy days or indoors?
A large share of UV radiation passes through clouds, so cloudy days still carry exposure, especially at midday and at higher altitudes. Window glass blocks most UVB but lets much UVA through, so people who sit near windows for long periods may benefit from daily sunscreen. Dermatologists commonly recommend daily application on exposed skin regardless of the weather.
Should I use sunscreen on babies?
For infants under six months, general guidance is to keep them out of direct sun and rely on shade, hats and lightweight protective clothing rather than sunscreen. For older babies and children, a broad spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or more is generally advised, often a mineral formula. Ask a pediatrician for advice specific to your child, especially if they have sensitive skin or eczema.
