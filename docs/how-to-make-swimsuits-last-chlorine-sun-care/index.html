How to Make Swimsuits Last: Chlorine, Sun and Care Tips

Swimsuits, swim trunks and board shorts often look worn out long before they tear: the fabric bags at the seat, colors fade, the elastic goes slack, or a once-opaque suit turns slightly see-through. These problems rarely come from age alone. They are caused by specific, predictable stressors, mainly pool chlorine, ultraviolet light, sunscreen and heat. Understanding how swim fabrics are built and what breaks them down helps you choose the right suit for how you swim and keep it in good shape for more seasons.

What Swimwear Fabric Is Made Of

Most fitted swimsuits are knitted from nylon (polyamide) or polyester blended with elastane, also called spandex. Nylon is soft, smooth and stretchy, which is why it dominates fashion swimwear. Polyester feels slightly firmer but holds color well and is more resistant to chlorine and sunlight. Elastane provides stretch and recovery, letting a suit hug the body and spring back after it is pulled on; fitted suits commonly contain a substantial share of it, often somewhere around 15 to 20 percent.

Elastane is also the weakest link. It is a segmented polyurethane fiber, and chlorine, heat, oils and UV light all attack its structure. As the elastane degrades, the fabric loses recovery, which shows up as sagging, rippling and a loose fit, and the knit can thin until it becomes less opaque.

Loose swim trunks and quick-dry board shorts are usually woven polyester or nylon with little or no elastane, which is why they tend to survive pool and sun exposure better than fitted suits, although their colors and water-repellent finishes still fade with heavy use.

How Chlorine, Sun and Sunscreen Break Swimwear Down

Chlorine in pools is a strong oxidizer, which is exactly why it is used to sanitize water. The same oxidizing action breaks down elastane and fades many dyes. Damage accumulates every time a suit is worn and then left to dry with pool water still in the fibers, so a suit used for regular laps in a chlorinated pool can wear out far sooner than one worn occasionally at the beach. Hot tubs are harsher still, combining high temperatures with concentrated sanitizers.

Ultraviolet light causes photodegradation, fading dyes and weakening both elastane and nylon over time. Bright colors and suits left drying in direct sun are most affected.

Sunscreens, tanning oils and some insect repellents can leave yellow or orange stains on light fabrics, and oils can soften and degrade elastane. Some sunscreen ingredients can also react with minerals in water to create stubborn discoloration, so rinsing quickly matters.

Friction completes the picture. Sitting on rough pool edges, concrete or wooden decks causes pilling and snags, especially on the seat of a suit, and these abraded areas are where thinning appears first.

Choosing Swimwear for How You Swim

The best fabric depends on where and how often you swim. For occasional beach and vacation use, nylon-elastane suits offer comfort and a wide range of styles, and with good care they can last several seasons.

For frequent pool swimming, look for suits described as chlorine resistant. These are usually made of 100 percent polyester, polyester with PBT (polybutylene terephthalate, a polyester with some natural stretch), or blends using elastane engineered for higher chlorine resistance. They feel firmer and stretch less, but they hold their shape and color much longer in treated water. A stated fiber composition is more informative than a vague label, so check the content tag.

For sun protection, rash guards and swim shirts may carry a UPF rating, which measures how much ultraviolet radiation the fabric blocks. Standards such as AS/NZS 4399 classify UPF 50 and above as excellent protection, meaning the fabric lets through about one fiftieth or less of UV radiation. Fabric density and color affect protection, and stretched or worn fabric generally offers less, so a snug but not overstretched fit preserves both comfort and coverage.

A Care Routine That Extends Swimsuit Life

The single most effective habit is rinsing a suit in cool, clean water as soon as possible after swimming, ideally before it dries. This flushes out chlorine, salt, sand and sunscreen before they keep acting on the fibers.

Wash swimwear by hand in cool water with a small amount of mild detergent, or use a gentle machine cycle inside a mesh laundry bag if the care label permits. Avoid hot water, bleach and fabric softener, which all degrade elastane. Do not wring or twist the suit; instead, roll it in a towel and press to remove excess water.

Dry swimwear flat or over a rack in the shade, never in a clothes dryer and never on a hot radiator. Heat is one of the fastest ways to destroy elastane.

Rotating between two or more suits gives each one time to dry completely and the fibers time to recover between wears, which is commonly recommended for regular swimmers. Sit on a towel rather than directly on rough surfaces, let sunscreen absorb before dressing, and store clean, dry suits flat and away from sunlight.

FAQ

Why is my swimsuit becoming see-through?
Thinning usually means the elastane and base fibers have been degraded by chlorine, heat or UV, and the knit has stretched and lost density. Areas under friction, such as the seat, are affected first. Once fabric has thinned this way it cannot be restored, so replacing the suit is the practical option.
Are chlorine-resistant swimsuits worth it?
For people who swim in chlorinated pools several times a week, they usually are. Polyester and PBT fabrics hold their shape and color far longer than standard nylon-elastane in treated water. They feel firmer and less stretchy, so for occasional beach wear a conventional suit is often more comfortable.
Can I put a swimsuit in the washing machine?
Some care labels allow a gentle, cool machine cycle, ideally with the suit inside a mesh laundry bag. Hand washing in cool water with mild detergent is gentler and is often recommended for padded or embellished suits. Always skip the dryer, because heat breaks down elastane quickly.
How do I remove sunscreen stains from swimwear?
Treat stains early. Rinse with cool water, then gently work a small amount of mild liquid detergent or dish soap into the mark and let it sit briefly before rinsing again. Avoid chlorine bleach, which damages elastane. Older yellow stains may be permanent, especially on white or pale fabrics.
