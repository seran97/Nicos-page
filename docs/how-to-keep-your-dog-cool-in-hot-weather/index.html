How to Keep Your Dog Cool in Hot Weather and Spot Heatstroke

Dogs cool themselves differently from people. They sweat only a little through their paw pads and rely mainly on panting, which moves air over moist surfaces in the mouth and airways. Panting becomes much less effective when the air is both hot and humid, which is why a dog can overheat on a day that feels merely warm to its owner. Heatstroke can develop quickly and can be fatal, so prevention matters. This guide explains which dogs are at higher risk, practical cooling strategies, how cooling gear works and how to recognize danger signs early.

Why Dogs Overheat and Which Dogs Are Most at Risk

A dog's normal body temperature is higher than a person's, generally around 101 to 102.5 degrees Fahrenheit. When heat builds up faster than panting can remove it, body temperature rises and organs can be damaged. Some dogs reach that point much sooner than others. Flat-faced, or brachycephalic, breeds such as bulldogs, pugs and boxers have shortened airways that make panting less efficient, and veterinarians consider them high risk. Dogs carrying excess weight, senior dogs, very young puppies, dogs with heart or respiratory disease, and dogs with thick double coats are also more vulnerable. Highly driven working and sporting dogs are at risk for a different reason: they often keep running or playing well past the point of overheating because they are excited. One of the most dangerous situations is a parked car, where interior temperatures can climb rapidly even on a mild day and with windows partly open. Never leave a dog in a parked vehicle in warm weather, even briefly. Humidity, direct sun and lack of shade all compound the risk.

Daily Habits That Keep Dogs Cool

Adjust routines before relying on gear. Walk during the coolest parts of the day, typically early morning and late evening, and shorten or slow down exercise during heat waves. Swap long runs or fetch sessions for gentle walks, sniffing games or indoor training, which provide mental exercise without much heat load. Always provide fresh water and shade; a collapsible bowl or portable water bottle makes it easy to offer frequent small drinks on outings. Indoors, keep at least one room cool with fans or air conditioning, and close curtains on sunny sides of the home. Protect paws from hot surfaces. Asphalt, concrete, sand and artificial turf can become far hotter than the air. A widely used check is to press the back of your hand on the surface for seven seconds; if it is too hot for your hand, it is too hot for paws. Walk on grass or shaded paths, or consider well-fitted booties for unavoidable hot pavement. Regular brushing removes loose undercoat and improves airflow, but avoid shaving double-coated breeds to the skin without professional advice, as the coat also protects against sunburn.

How Cooling Mats, Beds and Vests Work

Cooling products help, but they support rather than replace shade, water and limited exercise. Pressure-activated gel mats contain a gel that absorbs body heat when the dog lies on it and releases it once the dog gets up; they need no electricity or refrigeration but gradually warm up during long use and recover when left unused for a while. Water-filled mats work similarly by conducting heat away. Elevated mesh cot beds keep the dog off warm floors and let air circulate underneath. Supervise heavy chewers on gel mats, because punctured covers can expose the gel, and contact your veterinarian if any is swallowed. Evaporative cooling vests are soaked in water, wrung out and worn on walks; as the water evaporates, it draws heat away from the body. They work best in dry air, lose much of their effect in high humidity and must be rewetted once dry, since a dry vest can hold heat in. Choose light colors to reflect sunlight, check the fit around the chest and armpits as you would a harness, and never use cooling gear as a reason to extend activity in dangerous heat.

Recognizing Heatstroke and What to Do

Early signs of overheating include heavy, frantic panting, thick drool, a bright red or dark tongue and gums, restlessness and seeking shade or lying down repeatedly. As heat illness progresses, a dog may become weak, wobbly or confused, vomit, have diarrhea, collapse or have seizures. Heatstroke is an emergency. Move the dog to a shaded or air-conditioned place immediately and call a veterinary clinic while you begin cooling. Apply cool or lukewarm water, not ice-cold water, to the body, particularly the belly and inner legs, and place the dog in front of a fan to increase evaporation. Very cold water and ice can cause blood vessels in the skin to constrict, slowing heat loss. Do not leave wet towels draped over the dog for long periods, because they can trap heat. Offer small amounts of water if the dog is alert, but do not force it to drink. Take the dog to a veterinarian even if it seems to recover, because heatstroke can cause internal damage to organs and blood clotting that may only become apparent hours later.

FAQ

What temperature is too hot to walk a dog?
There is no single safe cutoff, because humidity, sun, breed, weight, age and fitness all matter. Risk rises noticeably as temperatures move into the high 70s and 80s Fahrenheit, especially with humidity. On hot days, walk early or late, keep it short, test pavement with your hand and watch for heavy panting.
Do cooling mats really work for dogs?
They can help a dog rest more comfortably by drawing heat from the body, especially on warm floors. Pressure-activated gel mats lose effect after long continuous use and recover when left alone. They are a comfort aid, not protection against heatstroke, so shade, water and a cool room remain essential.
Should I shave my double-coated dog for summer?
Many veterinarians and groomers advise against shaving double-coated breeds close to the skin, because the coat helps insulate against heat and protects against sunburn, and it may grow back unevenly. Regular brushing to remove dead undercoat is usually more helpful. Ask your veterinarian or a professional groomer for advice.
Can dogs get sunburn?
Yes. Dogs with thin, white or light-colored coats and pink skin on the nose, ears or belly can sunburn, as can dogs with shaved or sparse areas. Limit midday sun exposure and provide shade. If you want to use sunscreen, ask your veterinarian for a pet-safe product, as some human ingredients are unsafe if licked.
