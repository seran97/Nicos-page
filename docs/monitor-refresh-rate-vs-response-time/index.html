Refresh Rate vs Response Time: What Gaming Monitors Need

Gaming monitor spec sheets lead with two numbers that are easy to confuse: refresh rate, quoted in hertz, and response time, quoted in milliseconds. Both affect how smooth and sharp motion looks, but they describe different parts of the display pipeline, and a high figure in one cannot make up for a weak result in the other. Knowing what each number measures, and how marketing stretches them, makes it easier to compare monitors and set one up correctly.

What Refresh Rate Actually Measures

Refresh rate is how many times per second the monitor draws a new image, measured in hertz (Hz). A 60 Hz panel refreshes every 16.7 milliseconds, a 144 Hz panel every 6.9 ms, a 240 Hz panel every 4.2 ms and a 360 Hz panel every 2.8 ms. Those intervals are the frame time, and they explain why higher refresh rates feel more responsive: new information reaches the screen sooner, and moving objects advance in smaller steps between frames, so motion looks smoother and easier to track.

A high refresh rate only helps if the computer or console can deliver a matching frame rate. A graphics card producing 90 frames per second on a 240 Hz monitor still shows only 90 unique images each second; the monitor simply repeats some of them. The gains also shrink as numbers climb. Moving from 60 to 144 Hz cuts frame time by about 9.8 ms, while moving from 240 to 360 Hz saves only about 1.4 ms. That is why 144 to 165 Hz is a sensible target for most players, and very high refresh rates make the most sense for competitive shooters running at consistently high frame rates.

What Response Time Measures, and Why GtG Numbers Mislead

Response time describes how quickly an individual pixel changes from one shade to another. The most common figure is gray-to-gray (GtG), which measures the transition between two gray levels, usually timed from 10 percent to 90 percent of the change so the slow start and finish are ignored. If pixels cannot finish changing before the next refresh arrives, the result is a smeared trail behind moving objects, often called ghosting.

The headline number on the box is usually the fastest transition under the most aggressive overdrive setting, not an average across all transitions. Overdrive works by briefly applying extra voltage so the liquid crystals move faster, but pushed too far it overshoots the target shade and creates bright or dark halos known as inverse ghosting or overshoot. A monitor advertised at 1 ms may only reach that figure in a mode that looks worse in practice. The useful rule is that typical pixel transitions should comfortably fit inside the frame time: a 240 Hz monitor has about 4.2 ms per frame, so real-world transitions must be faster than that to look clean. Independent reviews with full response-time tables give a far more reliable picture.

Motion Blur, MPRT and Backlight Strobing

Even a monitor with instant pixel transitions shows some blur, because most displays use sample-and-hold: each frame stays lit for the entire refresh interval while your eyes keep moving to follow an object. The eye smears that static image across the retina, and the perceived blur is tied to how long each frame stays on screen. This is what Moving Picture Response Time (MPRT) tries to capture, and it is why raising the refresh rate reduces blur even on a very fast panel.

Backlight strobing, sold under names such as ULMB, ELMB or simply motion blur reduction, attacks the same problem differently. The backlight flashes briefly once per frame and stays dark in between, so each image is shown for only a fraction of the frame time, much like an old CRT. Motion becomes noticeably crisper, but brightness drops, some viewers notice flicker, and many monitors cannot strobe and use variable refresh rate at the same time. Strobing generally works best when the frame rate stays locked at or above the refresh rate. When a spec sheet quotes a very low MPRT figure, it is usually measured with strobing enabled, so check which mode the number refers to.

Variable Refresh Rate and Practical Setup Tips

Variable refresh rate (VRR) lets the monitor adjust its refresh timing to match the frames the graphics card actually produces. Without it, a mismatch between frame rate and refresh rate causes either screen tearing, where parts of two frames appear at once, or stutter and added input lag when traditional V-Sync forces the card to wait. Common implementations are AMD FreeSync, NVIDIA G-Sync and G-Sync Compatible, and the VRR feature of HDMI 2.1 used by current consoles. Each monitor supports VRR only within a range, for example 48 to 144 Hz, and low framerate compensation can repeat frames when a game drops below that floor.

A few settings make a large difference. Confirm the operating system is actually running the monitor at its maximum refresh rate, since many systems default to 60 Hz. Use a cable and port that support the full refresh rate at your resolution; DisplayPort or HDMI 2.1 is usually required for high refresh rates at 1440p and 4K. Try a middle overdrive setting first, because the strongest mode often adds overshoot. Finally, capping the frame rate a few frames below the maximum refresh rate is a common way to keep VRR active and avoid V-Sync latency.

FAQ

Is a higher refresh rate or a lower response time more important?
They matter together. Refresh rate sets how often new frames appear, while response time decides whether pixels finish changing before the next frame arrives. A 240 Hz monitor with slow pixel transitions will look smeared, and a fast panel limited to 60 Hz will still feel choppy. For most players, a 144 to 165 Hz monitor whose real transitions fit within its frame time is a balanced choice.
Does a 1 ms response time mean there is no ghosting?
No. The 1 ms figure is usually the best single gray-to-gray transition measured in the most aggressive overdrive mode, which may introduce overshoot artifacts. Average transitions across all shades are often slower, and dark transitions on some panel types are slower still. Independent measurements across many transitions, plus trying each overdrive level yourself, are better indicators of real motion clarity.
Is response time the same thing as input lag?
No. Input lag is the delay between the monitor receiving a signal and starting to display it, caused by image processing and scan-out. Response time is how long a pixel takes to physically change color once the change has started. A monitor can have fast pixels yet extra processing delay, which is why many monitors offer a game mode that disables some processing.
Can a console benefit from a 144 Hz monitor?
Some current-generation console games offer 120 Hz modes, so a monitor that accepts 120 Hz over HDMI 2.1 can show smoother motion in those titles. Many console games still target 30 or 60 frames per second, however, and gain nothing from extra refresh headroom. Check the console's supported output modes and whether the monitor supports HDMI VRR before paying for a higher refresh rate.
