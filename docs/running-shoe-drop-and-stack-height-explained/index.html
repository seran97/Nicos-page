Running Shoe Drop and Stack Height Explained

Running shoe specifications increasingly list two numbers in millimeters: drop and stack height. They describe the geometry of the midsole, and they influence how a shoe feels, how it rolls through each stride and which muscles and joints take more of the load. Neither number makes a shoe good or bad on its own, and research has not identified a single best value for everyone. Understanding what each one means helps you compare models, choose between traditional, low-drop and maximal designs, and avoid the most common mistake: changing too much at once.

What Heel-to-Toe Drop Means

Drop, also called offset or heel-to-toe drop, is the difference in height between the heel and the forefoot of a shoe, measured in millimeters. A shoe with 32 mm of material under the heel and 24 mm under the forefoot has an 8 mm drop. Traditional running shoes have often used drops of roughly 10 to 12 mm. Many current models sit in a moderate 6 to 8 mm range, low-drop shoes are around 4 mm or less, and zero-drop shoes keep the heel and forefoot level. Drop affects how the foot meets the ground and where load is concentrated. A higher drop slightly raises the heel, which tends to reduce the stretch and workload on the calf muscles and Achilles tendon. A lower drop asks those tissues to work through a greater range of motion and is sometimes associated with landing closer to the midfoot, although foot strike is influenced by many factors beyond the shoe. Importantly, drop is a static measurement taken without a runner in the shoe. Softer heel foam compresses more under load, so two shoes with the same listed drop can feel different once you are actually running in them.

What Stack Height Means and Why It Has Grown

Stack height is the total amount of material between your foot and the ground, usually quoted separately for the heel and forefoot and sometimes including the outsole and insole. Minimal shoes may have well under 20 mm, traditional trainers commonly fall in the mid-20s to low 30s, and maximal-cushion shoes can reach 35 to 40 mm or more at the heel. Taller stacks of modern lightweight foams can feel protective on long runs and soften the jarring sensation of hard surfaces, which appeals to many runners covering high mileage. There are trade-offs. A higher platform raises the foot further from the ground, which can reduce ground feel and make the shoe less stable on uneven terrain or tight corners, especially if the base is narrow. Manufacturers often counter this with wider soles, raised sidewalls or rocker shapes. Stack height has become important enough in racing that World Athletics caps sole thickness for road shoes used in elite competition at 40 mm, a rule introduced after the arrival of high-stack, carbon-plated designs. For most recreational runners this rule is irrelevant, but it shows how much stack height can affect performance and feel.

Matching Drop and Stack to Your Running

There is no universally correct combination, so the most practical approach is to match the shoe to your current habits, history and terrain. Runners who are comfortable in a familiar shoe and have no recurring problems have little reason to change its drop dramatically. People with a history of calf or Achilles irritation often find a moderate or higher drop more comfortable, while some runners with knee discomfort prefer a lower drop, though responses vary between individuals and these are tendencies rather than rules. For trail running, a lower stack usually improves stability and ground feel on technical, rocky paths, while a moderately cushioned shoe can suit smoother trails and long distances. For road training, many runners keep a cushioned daily trainer for easy miles and a lighter, sometimes lower-stack shoe for faster sessions. Carbon-plated racing shoes typically combine a high stack with a stiff plate and a rocker shape, which can feel efficient at faster paces but less stable at slow ones. Comfort during a short test run, on a flat surface and at your usual pace, remains one of the most useful signals available when choosing between options.

How to Transition Safely to a Different Drop or Stack

The biggest risk with drop and stack height is not choosing the wrong number but changing too quickly. Moving from a 10 to 12 mm shoe to a zero-drop or very low-drop model shifts extra work onto the calves, Achilles tendons and the small structures of the foot, and these tissues need weeks to adapt. A gradual approach is safer. Begin by using the new shoe for short, easy runs or walking, perhaps a small portion of your weekly volume, and increase that share slowly over several weeks while keeping your other shoes for the remainder. Avoid introducing new shoes in the same period as a big jump in mileage, hill work or speed sessions. A smaller step, such as moving from 10 mm to 6 or 8 mm before going lower, reduces the adjustment needed. Moving to a much higher stack also deserves some caution, particularly on trails or uneven ground where reduced stability matters. Some soreness in the calves when changing drop is common, but sharp pain, pain that worsens as you run or pain that persists between runs is a sign to cut back and consider advice from a qualified health professional.

FAQ

What is a good heel-to-toe drop for beginners?
Many beginners do well in a moderate drop of roughly 6 to 10 mm, which sits within the range most everyday trainers use and does not demand major adaptation from the calves and Achilles. Comfort matters more than the exact number, and switching later to a very different drop should be done gradually.
Is a zero-drop shoe better for running form?
Zero-drop shoes may encourage some runners to land closer to the midfoot, but research does not show they are better for everyone or that they prevent injuries. They place more load on the calves and Achilles tendon, so they suit some runners well and others poorly. Any switch should be gradual.
Does higher stack height mean more cushioning?
Usually there is more material to compress, but the feel also depends on foam type and firmness. A high stack of firm foam can feel less soft than a moderate stack of a very soft compound. Higher stacks can also reduce stability, so test the shoe on the surfaces you normally run.
Do drop and stack height matter for walking?
They matter less than for running because walking produces lower impact forces, but they still affect comfort. Very high or very low drops can feel unusual at first, and very high stacks may feel less stable on uneven ground. For walking, comfort, fit and a stable base usually deserve the most weight.
