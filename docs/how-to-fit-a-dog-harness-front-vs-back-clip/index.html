How to Fit a Dog Harness: Front-Clip vs Back-Clip Explained

A harness spreads pressure across the chest and shoulders instead of the throat, which is why many trainers and veterinarians suggest one for dogs that pull, small breeds with delicate tracheas, and flat-faced breeds. The benefit only holds if the harness fits well and the leash attaches in the right place. A loose harness can be backed out of in seconds, while a tight or poorly shaped one can rub the armpits and restrict the way the shoulders move. This guide covers how to measure, how to check the fit, and how to choose between clip positions.

How to Measure Your Dog Before Choosing a Size

Harness sizing is based on body measurements, not weight or breed, because two dogs of the same weight can have very different chest shapes. Use a soft tailor's tape and measure with your dog standing. The most important number is chest girth: wrap the tape around the widest part of the rib cage, just behind the front legs, snug but not compressing the fur. Next, measure the neck at its base, close to the shoulders, rather than high up under the jaw where a collar might sit. Some manufacturers also ask for the length from the base of the neck to the girth line. Write the numbers down and compare them with each product's own size chart, since sizes labeled small, medium or large vary widely between makers. If your dog falls between two sizes, the adjustable range matters more than the label: choose the size where your dog's measurements sit near the middle of the adjustment range, which leaves room to loosen for a winter coat or tighten if the straps stretch. Puppies should be remeasured every few weeks while they grow.

The Two-Finger Rule and Other Fit Checks

Once the harness is on, slide two flat fingers under every strap, including the chest strap, belly strap and neck opening. They should fit with light resistance; if you can fit your whole hand, it is too loose, and if two fingers will not slide in, it is too tight. Then check the position of the straps. The chest strap should sit on the breastbone, not low on the soft belly. The girth strap should sit about a hand's width behind the front legs so it does not dig into the armpits, where friction sores often start. Watch your dog walk and trot. The shoulders should move freely with a full forward stride. Finally, do an escape test in a safe, enclosed area: gently pull the leash forward and to the sides as a dog would when backing away. If the neck opening slides over the ears, tighten the neck strap. After the first few walks, part the fur and check under the legs and behind the elbows for redness, hair loss or damp patches, which signal rubbing that needs an adjustment or a different design.

Front-Clip vs Back-Clip vs Dual-Clip Harnesses

Where the leash attaches changes how a harness behaves. A front-clip harness has its ring on the chest. When the dog pulls, the leash tension turns the dog sideways back toward the handler, which reduces the forward leverage of pulling. This makes front-clip designs a common training tool for strong pullers, although the leash can tangle around the front legs if it is long or slack. A back-clip harness has its ring between the shoulder blades. It is easy to put on, keeps the leash clear of the legs and suits calm walkers, small dogs and dogs with throat sensitivity. Its drawback is that it can make pulling more comfortable, so some dogs pull harder, much like a sled harness. A dual-clip harness offers both rings, letting you use the front ring during training or on busy streets and the back ring for relaxed walks or with a hands-free leash. Some handlers clip a double-ended leash to both rings for extra control. None of these designs replaces training; a harness manages pulling while you teach loose-leash walking with rewards.

Strap Shape, Materials and When to Re-Check

Look at how the straps cross the chest. Y-shaped designs, where the straps meet at the breastbone and leave the shoulder points uncovered, are generally considered less restrictive than designs with a straight horizontal strap across the front of the shoulders, which can shorten stride in some dogs. For active dogs, check that buckles and adjusters sit away from the armpits and spine. Padding helps on short-coated breeds but adds warmth in summer, so breathable mesh is worth considering in hot climates. Reflective trim improves visibility on early or late walks. Strong dogs need metal rings rather than plastic ones, and quick-release buckles should click securely. Re-check the fit whenever your dog gains or loses weight, grows a thick seasonal coat, gets clipped, or after washing, since webbing can shrink or stretch. Remove the harness at home, especially if your dog is unsupervised, because straps can catch on furniture or crate wire. If your dog has a heart, spinal or orthopedic condition, ask your veterinarian which harness style is appropriate.

FAQ

Is a harness better than a collar for dogs that pull?
For many dogs, yes. A harness moves leash pressure away from the throat, which matters for small breeds, dogs prone to tracheal problems and flat-faced breeds. A front-clip harness can also reduce pulling mechanically. Many owners still keep a flat collar on for ID tags and use the harness only for walks.
How tight should a dog harness be?
Snug enough that your dog cannot back out of it, yet loose enough to slide two flat fingers under every strap. The chest and girth straps should not shift much as your dog walks, and they should never dig into the armpits or leave marks on the skin after removal.
Can my dog wear a harness all day?
It is generally better to remove it when not walking. Constant wear increases the chance of rubbing, matted fur and skin irritation, and loose straps can snag on furniture or crates. A plain collar with ID tags is usually a safer choice for time at home.
Why does my dog pull more in a back-clip harness?
A back-clip harness lets a dog lean into the chest straps comfortably, similar to how sled dogs pull, so it does not discourage pulling. A front-clip ring redirects the dog sideways when it pulls. Pair either style with consistent reward-based loose-leash training for lasting results.
